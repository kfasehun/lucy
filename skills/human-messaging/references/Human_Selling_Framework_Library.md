# Human Selling Framework Library

A working reference for Brightcove outbound and follow-up messaging. This is an original synthesis of publicly documented sales methodologies — not reproduced book text — built specifically to ground a messaging approach that treats the person on the other end as a person, not a contact record.

Note on the "POOL framework": the closest real match is the **PULL framework** by Rob Snyder (Fellow, Harvard Innovation Labs). There is no widely documented "POOL" methodology — likely a mishearing. PULL is included below.

---

## 1. PULL Framework — Rob Snyder

**Core idea:** A buyer with real PULL doesn't need to be convinced — they pull the product out of your hands, because it unblocks something they're already prioritizing right now, for a reason that makes waiting weird or costly.

**Diagnostic questions before writing anything:**
- Is there a project already on this person's to-do list, or would we be creating one from scratch?
- Why would this be urgent *now* versus six months from now?
- Would it be strange, internally, for them *not* to act on this?

**Operational takeaway:** If we can't answer these for a given contact, we shouldn't be asking for their time yet — we should be asking questions that surface whether PULL exists at all. A message with no evidence of PULL should not contain a meeting ask.

Source: [The PULL Framework, In Detail](https://thephysicsofstartups.substack.com/p/the-pull-framework-in-detail), [The PULL theory of business](https://thephysicsofstartups.substack.com/p/the-pull-theory-of-business)

## 2. Gap Selling — Keenan

**Core idea:** People don't buy products, they buy the distance closed between where they are (current state) and where they want to be (future state). The rep's job is to make that gap visible — not to describe the product.

**Current state has five parts:** environment, problem, impact, root cause, emotion. Most reps stop at "problem." The emotion and root-cause layers are where the actual decision lives.

**Critical rule:** the future state has to come from the buyer's own words, not the seller's script. If we're the ones describing what their future should look like, we're pitching. If they're describing it, we're selling.

**Operational takeaway:** Ask, don't assert. Never write "you probably struggle with X" — ask what's actually true and let them tell us. Follow-ups should invite their words back, not restate our value prop.

Source: [Gong: Complete Guide to Gap Selling](https://www.gong.io/blog/gap-selling)

## 3. Sandler Selling System — David Sandler

**Core idea:** Reframe the interaction as mutual qualification, not persuasion. The rep is allowed to walk away from a bad fit — that permission is what makes the conversation honest.

**The Pain Funnel** — three layers, in order:
1. **Technical pain** — what's actually broken or slow
2. **Business/financial pain** — what it's costing the org
3. **Personal pain** — how it affects *this person* specifically

The personal layer is usually what drives the final decision, and it's the layer almost every SDR sequence skips entirely.

**Up-Front Contract** — before asking for time, be explicit about purpose, their agenda, our agenda, time required, and what a "no" looks like. A contract that doesn't allow for "no" isn't a real contract, and prospects can feel that.

**Operational takeaway:** Every message should try to reach toward the personal-pain layer, not just restate business pain the prospect already knows. And any meeting ask should make it easy and safe to say no.

Source: [Gong: The Sandler Pain Funnel](https://www.gong.io/blog/sandler-pain-funnel), [Sandler Method: Upfront Contracts](https://www.hyperbound.ai/blog/sandler-method-upfront-contracts-permission-no)

## 4. Tactical Empathy — Chris Voss (Never Split the Difference)

**Core idea:** Built for hostage negotiation, transfers directly to B2B: a guarded, busy person won't engage until they feel accurately understood, not persuaded.

**Labeling** — name the emotion you're observing, without judgment: *"It sounds like this hasn't been a priority"* or *"It seems like the timing's been off."* Naming an emotion accurately tends to defuse it and open the door to a real answer.

**Mirroring** — repeat the last 2-3 words of what someone just said or wrote, with genuine curiosity, to invite elaboration rather than filling the silence ourselves.

**Operational takeaway:** When a prospect has declined or gone quiet, the strongest next message often *names what's happening* ("I've asked for time twice now and that's probably not what's useful") rather than pretending it didn't happen and asking a third time.

Source: [Tactical Empathy in Sales](https://www.upwardspiralgroup.com/blog/tactical-empathy-in-sales-how-to-build-trust-and-connect-with-your-prospects), [Mirroring, Labeling, and Tactical Empathy](https://attackonjack.substack.com/p/mirror-labeling-and-the-practice)

## 5. Josh Braun — "Teaching, Not Telling"

**Core idea:** Most prospects assume a seller has an ulterior motive the moment they sense a pitch. The goal is to never trigger that "zone of resistance" in the first place.

**Principles:** understanding over convincing, curiosity over certainty, silence over filling space, abundance over neediness (write like the outcome doesn't determine our worth), vulnerability over polish.

**The "before story"** — describe the cost of doing nothing / what life looks like right before someone finally acts, rather than leading with ROI or features. This is what makes a prospect feel understood rather than marketed to.

**Operational takeaway:** If a message would make the sender wince if read aloud to the prospect's face, don't send it. No "just circling back," no "wanted to touch base," no invented urgency.

Source: [Josh Braun — Stop Persuading](https://joshbraun.com/), [Learn Prospecting: Teaching Not Telling](https://joshbraun.com/learn/prospecting/)

## 6. MEDDIC — The Champion

**Core idea:** A champion isn't just someone who likes us — they're someone with internal credibility who sells on our behalf when we're not in the room. Our job is to make *them* look good, not just to get their signature.

**Operational takeaway:** Give the contact things they can forward or say internally that make them look informed and proactive to their own boss — a sharp insight, a defensible number, a low-risk next step — rather than only asking them for time or access.

Source: [MEDDICC: Champion Definition](https://meddicc.com/what-is-meddpicc/champion), [iSEEit: What is a MEDDIC Champion](https://now.iseeit.com/how-to-identify-a-meddic-champion/)

## 7. Modern Outbound Benchmarks (2025-2026)

- Personalized, small-batch outreach (under ~50 recipients) averages meaningfully higher reply rates than 1,000+ volume blasts — roughly 3x in recent industry data.
- The strongest cold opens lead with one specific, verifiable observation about the person or company — a launch, a platform, a public statement — not a generic industry claim.
- Authentic, slightly imperfect voice consistently outperforms polished, corporate phrasing. The informal test: *if you wouldn't say it to someone at a bar, cut it from the email.*
- Response speed compounds trust — replying fast, in a human register, matters more than a longer, more "complete" message sent later.

Source: [Founder-led outbound strategies](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/founder-led-sales-framework), [Starnus: Outbound Without an SDR](https://starnus.com/blog/outbound-sales-solo-founder)

## Synthesis: What This Means for a Brightcove Message

Before writing anything, answer three questions about the specific person:

1. **What's their PULL?** Is there a real, time-bound reason this matters to them right now, or are we inventing urgency?
2. **What's the personal layer?** Beyond the business case, how does this affect *them* — their time, their credibility, their relationship with their boss?
3. **What would make them look good?** What could we hand them that they'd be proud to forward internally, rather than something that asks more of them?

Then write the message so that:
- It names what's actually happening in the relationship, if something's off (a decline, silence, repeated asks) — don't pretend otherwise.
- It asks at least one real question and leaves room for "no."
- It never uses hedge phrases that exist only to sound busy or important ("just circling back," "wanted to touch base," "following up on my last email," "not a generic pitch").
- The ask, if there is one, is the smallest version of the ask that still moves things forward.
