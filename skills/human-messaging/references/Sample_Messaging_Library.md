# Sample Messaging Library

Reference examples for the human-messaging skill, one per common scenario. Each follows the same
rules: no em dashes, no phrases that disclaim their own motive ("not trying to sell you," "no
pressure"), no exposed internal uncertainty, one real question or the smallest possible ask, and
room to say no. Swap in real names, companies, and specifics before sending, none of this should
go out as-is.

---

## 1. Net new outreach (cold, real trigger)

**When to use:** First-ever contact, and there's a specific, verifiable reason this person matters
right now (a launch, a platform change, a role change, a public statement). Never send this without
a real trigger, if there's no trigger, don't pretend there is one.

> Subject: Saw the Samsung TV+ launch, [Name]
>
> [Name], saw [Company] just launched on Samsung TV+. Curious how the team is handling delivery
> across that many destinations at once.
>
> Worth 20 minutes to compare notes? Happy to share what we're seeing work for similar rollouts.

---

## 2. Picking up after a missed or unconfirmed meeting

**When to use:** A meeting was supposed to happen (call, StreamTV table, etc.) and there's no clear
record either way of whether it did. Never ask the prospect to confirm this for you, it makes you
look like you don't track your own meetings. Reference the real touchpoint and move forward.

> Subject: Following up from StreamTV, [Name]
>
> [Name], wanted to reconnect after StreamTV.
>
> What's top of mind for [Company]'s [content distribution / streaming setup] right now? Worth 30
> minutes next week to dig in?

---

## 3. You personally missed it, a colleague covered

**When to use:** You know exactly what happened, you weren't there, someone else stepped in. Own
it plainly, don't over-explain or apologize repeatedly.

> Subject: Sorry I missed you at StreamTV, [Name]
>
> [Name], I had something come up around StreamTV and my colleague stepped in for me. Wanted to
> reach out directly now that I'm back.
>
> How did that conversation go on your end, and is it worth picking up together?

---

## 4. A meeting happened, real follow-up

**When to use:** The meeting is confirmed to have happened. Reference something specific from it,
don't generic-pitch. If you don't have a real detail from the call, get one before sending this.

> Subject: Following up on [specific topic discussed]
>
> [Name], good talking through [specific thing they mentioned] earlier. Wanted to follow up on
> [specific next step discussed] before it falls off the radar.
>
> Does [specific next step] still make sense on your end, or has something shifted?

---

## 5. Declined a meeting, asked for something else instead

**When to use:** They said no to a call but asked for information, a one-pager, a deck. Give them
exactly what they asked for, no strings, then offer a much smaller next step than the original ask.

> Subject: [What they asked for]
>
> [Name], here's [the thing they asked for].
>
> Take a look and let me know what's relevant or if anything raises questions. Happy to do 15
> minutes live if it's easier to walk through, otherwise just reply here.

---

## 6. Repeated declines, still pursuing

**When to use:** Two or more real declines from the same person. Do not repeat the same ask a
third time reworded, that reads as pressure regardless of phrasing. Name the pattern, ask a
genuine question, or stop.

> [Name], I've asked twice now for time on your calendar. That's clearly not what's useful to you
> right now, so I'll stop.
>
> If it's still relevant: what's actually driving [the original question they asked]? That's
> usually not an idle question, so I'm curious what's behind it.

---

## 7. Multiple no-response follow-ups, considering a "breakup" message

**When to use:** Three or more touches with zero response, especially if earlier ones used generic
language ("bumping this," "circling back"). This is a different message type, not a fourth
rephrasing of the same ask. Give a clean, low-pressure exit.

> Subject: Should I stop reaching out, [Name]?
>
> [Name], this is my fourth note on this and I haven't heard back once, so I'll take the hint.
>
> If the timing's just off and you want to pick this up later, say the word and I'll check back
> down the road instead of your inbox. Otherwise I'll leave it here.

---

## 8. Serial rescheduler (keeps saying "maybe," never commits)

**When to use:** Multiple proposed times, declines, or "maybe" responses, no firm commitment ever
lands. Different problem than silence, don't use the breakup message here. Ask directly if it's
worth continuing, with a real deadline-shaped ask.

> Subject: Worth still doing, [Name]?
>
> [Name], we've moved this a few times now, sometimes with a "maybe," and I'd rather check in than
> keep proposing new dates.
>
> If it's still worth doing, pick a day next week you know you'll actually be free. If not, no
> hard feelings, just let me know.

---

## 9. They explicitly said "not right now, we'll reach out"

**When to use:** A clear, specific decline with a stated reason and a stated preference for how
they'll re-engage. Do not push again with a pitch, that reads as not listening. Acknowledge and
stand down gracefully, this builds trust for later even though it feels counterintuitive.

> Subject: Understood, [Name]
>
> [Name], got it, I'll stand down on my end. If anything changes on your side, my info's below
> whenever it's useful.

---

## 10. A meeting was set (verbally, on a call) but never actually got scheduled

**When to use:** Dialer/call notes show a meeting was agreed to, but no calendar invite or email
ever followed. This is a dropped ball on your side, not theirs. Own it plainly and fix it.

> Subject: Let's actually get this on the calendar
>
> [Name], we spoke a few weeks back about [Company] and Brightcove, and a meeting never actually
> got scheduled after that call. My mistake.
>
> What's a good day next week for 30 minutes?

---

## 11. Warm referral or intro request

**When to use:** Someone pointed you to a different contact, or you want a current contact to make
an introduction. Make it as close to zero-effort as possible for the person doing the favor.

> [Name], thanks for pointing me in the right direction. Would you feel comfortable making a quick
> intro to [right person]? Happy to draft a two-line note you could just forward, so it's no extra
> work on your end.

---

## General checklist before sending anything

- Does this reference something real and specific, not a generic industry claim?
- Is there exactly one question or ask, and is it the smallest version that still moves things
  forward?
- Would this be uncomfortable to say to the person's face? If yes, cut it.
- Does it expose any internal uncertainty (did we meet, did you get my last email, are you still
  there) instead of just moving forward?
- Any em dashes, "not trying to," "no pressure," "just checking in," or "bumping this"? Cut them.
- If proposing a time, does it account for the actual calendar, holidays, and weekends?
